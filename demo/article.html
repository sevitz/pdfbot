The quiet life of a borrowed word

Most of the words a child meets in a book arrived in English from somewhere else. Following a few of them home says a lot about how a vocabulary grows.

By the Wordanator team · 9 minute read · Sample text for the pdfbot demo

Open any children's novel and count the words that were born in English. It will not take long. The ship that is becalmed sits on a word from Greek by way of French; the quay it ties up to came from Old French; even the anchor dropped from it was borrowed from Latin, which had it from Greek before that. English has always been a magpie, and the books children read are full of the shiny things it picked up.

That matters for how a child learns them. A borrowed word often arrives with no relatives in the reader's head. Starboard at least contains board; bazaar contains nothing a nine year old already knows. Words like that have to be met several times, in a story that makes them matter, before they settle.

A word with no relatives has to be met three or four times before it stays.

Some borrowings are so recent you can almost watch them land. Tsunami came into everyday English from Japanese in living memory; in the original it is written 津波, harbour wave, a description so exact that English simply kept it. A child who learns that the word means harbour wave tends to remember both the word and the idea.1

Others have been here so long nobody notices. Shampoo began as a Hindi word for pressing or kneading, came to England with a Bengali entrepreneur who opened a steam bath in Brighton, and spent a century meaning a massage before it settled on hair.2 Hardly anyone reading a bath-time scene needs to know that, but the few who are told it never forget the word.

What this means for a word list

The practical lesson for a family word list is that borrowed words deserve a little story of their own. The definition says what the word means; a sentence about where it came from gives the child something to hang it on. That is why the quiz grades an answer yellow rather than red when the idea is right but the explanation stops short: the hook is there, and the word is on its way in.

It also suggests a game. When a strange word turns up at the dinner table, guess where it came from before looking it up. The guesses are usually wrong, and that is half the fun. Kayak really is from Greenlandic Inuit. Ketchup probably came from a Hokkien word for fish sauce, which surprises almost everyone.

Here is a short sentence in Japanese to finish, so that the PDF's text layer has some characters well outside Latin to carry: 言葉は旅をする。 Words travel.

  1. The Japanese characters are 津 (harbour) and 波 (wave).
  2. The steam bath was Mahomed's Baths, opened on the Brighton seafront in 1814.
